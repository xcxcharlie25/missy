Design a new retail pharmacy network, one client at a time

Not a reshuffle of members inside today's network. We start from every pharmacy in the country that any Optum Rx client used in 2025, price every one of the client's members' medicines at every pharmacy near them, and let the optimizer pick the network and the assignments that cost the least while keeping access and quality.

What goes in

  • 2025 claims, the whole book of business, every client.
  • From them: a pharmacy universe, a price list per pharmacy and drug, and the client's member demand.
  • Business weights: value of a changed visit, value of a mile.

What the model decides

  • Which pharmacies are in the network (one yes/no per pharmacy).
  • Which pharmacy each member is assigned to (one yes/no per member and candidate).
  • Subject to: one pharmacy per member, within reach, capacity, a quality floor, chains in or out by state.

What comes out

  • The new network and the assignment list.
  • Drug bill under the new network vs what the client paid in 2025, like for like.
  • Miles added, visits moved, quality before and after.
Real and synthetic. Everything above is real 2025 data and stays in house. The only synthetic element in the model is the per pharmacy network cost, a placeholder carried from the original code until contract figures arrive. D-Wave receives a synthetic copy of the data with the same tables and column names, never the real data.

From 2025 claims to three tables

Every paid claim of 2025 is read once. We keep only sums per pharmacy and per pharmacy and drug, so a billion claims become tables we can open. Rejected and reversed claims are dropped first.

Pharmacy universe

one row per pharmacy, all clients

  • Who: name, chain, NCPDP number
  • Where: state, metro, coordinates
  • What kind: type and class (next page)
  • How big: fills in 2025 and capacity
  • Used by the client in 2025: yes or no

Price list

one row per pharmacy, drug, generic or brand

  • Ingredient cost per unit
  • Dispensing fee per fill
  • Where the price came from: the client's own claims, all clients, or the chain
  • How many claims and units are behind it

Client members

one row per member, and one per member and drug

  • Where the member lives (coordinates)
  • Each drug: fills, quantity, days supply
  • Which pharmacy they use today, how many visits
  • What the client paid for them in 2025 (the baseline)
In plain words: the pharmacy side is built from every client, because a new network must be able to choose pharmacies the client never used, and we need a real price for them. The member side is one client only, because that client's members are the demand we are placing.

The pharmacy universe

Every pharmacy that any client used in 2025, 86,732 of them. Phase 1 works with the retail ones. Within retail, the dispensing class is the "kind" of pharmacy a member is matched within.

Type, then class

What each pharmacy row carries

LocationState, metro area, latitude and longitude (median of its claims). Distances to members are computed from the coordinates, never from the address text.
ChainUsed for the chain price fallback and for the "a chain is in or out by state" rule.
Size and capacityFills in 2025 across all clients. We assume that was 90% of what the pharmacy can take: capacity = fills ÷ 0.9. The 10% headroom can be reserved for members in areas not yet covered.
Used by the clientFlag: at least one fill by the client in 2025. The others are candidates priced from other clients' claims. FedEx: 39,864 pharmacies used, 46,868 candidates it never used.
MoneyTotals paid there in 2025, all clients and the client. Context and baseline, not the price (the price comes from the price list).
Matching rule: a member is moved only within the class they use today. A member who used a Chain pharmacy gets a Chain pharmacy; a member who used two classes gets one pharmacy of each.
Guarantee: a member whose only pharmacy is a Government or Alternate Dispensing Site also gets the closest normal pharmacy assigned, with zero fills, so nobody is left without a regular option.

How a drug is priced at a pharmacy

Cost means what the client pays: ingredient cost plus dispensing fee. Both come from real paid claims. Three scenarios decide whose claims we average.

A

The client used this pharmacy for this drug

Even one fill by the client is enough. The price is the client's own average at that pharmacy.

Example: the client's members filled 7 claims of a generic tablet at store 123 in 2025, 420 tablets, $40.32 ingredient paid. Price = 40.32 ÷ 420 = $0.096 per tablet. Source: client.
B

The client never filled it there, other clients did

The price is the average over all clients' claims at that pharmacy. Same store, same contract, other members' receipts.

Example: no claim by the client at store 456, but 19 claims from other clients, 1,050 tablets, $98.70 paid. Price = 98.70 ÷ 1,050 = $0.094 per tablet. Source: all clients.
C

Nobody filled it there

We never assume a pharmacy cannot dispense a drug. We take the chain's average for that drug, and if the chain never filled it, the average across all pharmacies.

Example: store 789 belongs to Chain A; Chain A stores filled the drug 3,100 times. Price = Chain A's paid ÷ Chain A's tablets. Source: chain.

The averaging, always volume weighted

Price per unit = total money paid ÷ total units dispensed. Fee per fill = total fees ÷ number of fills. Never an average of averages.

Three rules that keep it honest

  • Generic and brand are never mixed. The drug key is the GPI (molecule, form, strength) plus generic or brand. Brand Amoxil and generic amoxicillin share a GPI but get separate prices.
  • Only paid claims count. Rejected and reversed rows are dropped before anything is summed.
  • The source travels with the price. Every cost in the results says whether it rests on the client's own claims, other clients' claims, or a chain fallback.
Why other clients' prices are valid: pricing is passthrough. The client pays what the pharmacy is paid, and the pharmacy's rate comes from its chain contract, not from the client. Where the client and other clients both have claims at a store, the two averages can be compared to prove it.

Members, and how they connect to pharmacies

One client is one carrier id (, CARRIER_ID = ). Its accounts and employer groups share one network, so the model runs for the whole client at once and is solved metro by metro.

Client level

  • One client = one carrier id ().
  • Accounts and employer groups under it are labels for reporting; they share pharmacies and one network.
  • Pharmacy prices are set per chain contract, not per client, which is why the universe is built from all clients.

Member level

  • Location: median of the member's coordinates on their claims.
  • Demand: per drug (GPI + generic/brand), fills and quantity in 2025, within the dispensing class used.
  • Today: the pharmacy with the most fills, and the number of visits (distinct fill dates).

Baseline

  • What the client actually paid at retail for each member in 2025: ingredient cost plus fees.
  • The new network is compared against it with the same members, same drugs, same quantities.
  • Only the pharmacy changes, and every pharmacy's price is what it was really paid.

The cost function, in formula and in words

The optimizer minimizes one total. Four terms: the drug bill, the cost of the network itself, disruption to members, and distance. The first is data; the last three are business judgement made explicit.

Formula

Cost of member m at pharmacy p C[m,p] = Σ over the member's drugs d of price[p,d] × quantity[m,d] + fee[p,d] × fills[m,d] Total to minimize Total = Σ C[m,p] · a[m,p] drug bill + Σ N[p] · x[p] network cost + A · (visits moved) disruption + λ · (extra miles) access Decisions x[p] = 1 if pharmacy p is in the network a[m,p] = 1 if member m is assigned to p
m, p, da member, a pharmacy, a drug (GPI plus generic or brand)
price[p,d], fee[p,d]from the price list, scenario A, B or C
quantity[m,d], fills[m,d]the member's 2025 demand for that drug
N[p]yearly cost of keeping pharmacy p in the network
A, λbusiness weights: value of a changed visit, value of a mile

Words

Drug bill
What the client would pay in a year if every member filled their 2025 medicines at the pharmacy they are assigned to, at that pharmacy's real prices. The big number.
Network cost placeholder
A fixed yearly cost for every pharmacy kept in the network (administration, credentialing). Synthetic values from the original code until contract figures arrive; can be set to zero.
Disruption
A price A we put on every visit a member would have to make at a different pharmacy than today. Keeps the model from moving people for pennies.
Access
A price λ per extra mile a member travels compared to today, times their visits. Keeps the network close to where people live.

The rules the optimizer must respect

One pharmacy eachEvery member gets exactly one pharmacy in every class they use. Only pharmacies that are in the network can be assigned.
Within reachOnly pharmacies inside the access radius of the member are candidates.
CapacityFills assigned to a pharmacy cannot exceed its capacity (2025 fills ÷ 0.9).
Quality floorThe fill weighted quality of the assigned pharmacies cannot drop below today's level. Quality is measured by adherence on maintenance drugs, from real fills.
Chains by stateA chain's stores in a state are in or out together, the way contracts are signed.

What the search space looks like

Two layers of yes/no decisions, and the answer to one layer changes the best answer to the other. That coupling is what makes the problem hard, and what quantum hardware is being tested on.

Network decisions, retail
62,378

members × 7≈ 700K
FedEx: about 100K members × 7 realistic candidates each. Every 10K members ≈ 62K decisions.

≈ 640M

Why it is not a lookup

  • Cheapest for one is not cheapest for all. Send every member to their cheapest pharmacy and you open thousands of pharmacies, each with a network cost and a capacity. Close pharmacies and members must move, which costs disruption and miles.
  • Decisions interact. Opening one store changes the best choice for every member nearby, and through capacity, for members further away. Chains in or out by state couple whole regions.
  • Many near equal answers. On a toy version with 4,096 possible networks, 338 looked best to any single change, yet only 2 were truly best under swaps. Real sizes have vastly more such traps.

The landscape

A solver that only accepts improving single changes stops in the first valley it finds. The real question is how far that valley is from the best one, and how fast each method gets out.

Classical, real data

Pyomo and HiGHS solve the 0/1 program metro by metro in house, with a proof of how far the answer can be from optimal (the gap). This is the production path.

D-Wave, synthetic data

The same model is sent to the hybrid constrained quadratic solver on a synthetic copy of the data (same tables, same columns, invented members and pharmacies). We compare answer quality and time against the classical run at matching sizes.

What we learn

Whether the quantum hybrid finds better valleys, or finds them faster, as the problem grows from one metro to the whole clientall of to the whole book, where the classical path runs out of room.
